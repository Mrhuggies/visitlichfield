Take a moment to look across the water. This is Minster Pool, a serene reflection of the Cathedral’s spires. But this "natural" looking pool is actually one of the oldest man-made features in the city, dating back over 900 years.

Historians used to think this was just a medieval fish pond. However, when the pool was dredged in the 19th century, engineers discovered something fascinating: the pool likely sits in a massive hollow created by quarrying sandstone to build the original Norman Cathedral in 1085. To make use of the hole, they dammed a boggy stream to power a corn mill, which stood on Dam Street for nearly 800 years.

Don't let the ducks fool you—this pool was once a lethal moat. During the Civil War sieges, the pool was the primary defense for the Royalists inside the Cathedral Close. In fact, when the pool was cleaned out years later, workers found dozens of cannonballs and shells buried in the mud—remnants of the heavy fire exchanged across these very waters.

If you think the pool looks a bit like the one in London’s Hyde Park, that’s no accident. In 1772, the local poet Anna Seward—known as the "Swan of Lichfield"—was so inspired by the Serpentine in London that she campaigned to have Minster Pool landscaped into this elegant, curving shape, creating the "New Walk" you’re standing on today.

Here’s a quirky bit of legal history: In 1896, a worker named Mr. Sharman was cleaning out the mud here and found two gold rings. He tried to keep them, but the Water Company sued him. The resulting court case, South Staffordshire Water Co v Sharman, is still taught to law students today to decide who owns "treasures" found on private land!

Whether you’re here for the views or a bit of public speaking at the Speakers' Corner just ahead, remember that you’re walking on a thousand years of Lichfield’s history.
