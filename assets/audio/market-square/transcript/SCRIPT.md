You are standing in the historic heart of Lichfield. For over eight hundred years, this square has been the city’s meeting place, its marketplace, and—in darker times—the site of its most public dramas.

Take a look at the two bronze figures watching over the square. On one side, we have Samuel Johnson, the dictionary maker, looking heavy and contemplative. Across from him stands his biographer and friend, James Boswell. It’s the perfect place for them to rest, as the house where Johnson was born in 1709 sits right there on the corner.

But look toward the ground near the center of the square. This area hides a grim past. Lichfield was one of the last places in England to burn people at the stake for their religious beliefs. In 1612, a man named Edward Wightman was executed here for heresy. He holds the unenviable title of being the very last person in England to be burned at the stake. Today, a small plaque marks the spot where the fires once burned.

There is a more touching story here, too. Late in his life, a famous and elderly Dr. Johnson returned to this square. He stood bareheaded in the pouring rain for hours, right where his father’s bookstall used to be. He was performing a "penance" because, as a teenager, he had pridefully refused to help his father work the stall. Even at the height of his fame, he never forgot the debt he owed to this square.

If you’re here on a Tuesday, Friday, or Saturday, you’ll see the stalls still standing much as they have since King Edward the First granted the city a market charter in 1291. From medieval merchants to modern-day shoppers, the rhythm of life in Lichfield hasn't changed all that much.

Whether you’re grabbing a coffee or heading into the Guildhall nearby, take a moment to appreciate the centuries of footsteps that have crossed these cobblestones before you.
