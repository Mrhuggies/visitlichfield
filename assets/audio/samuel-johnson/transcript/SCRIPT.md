You’re standing before a house that was once the center of the literary world—though it certainly didn't start that way. This tall, timber-framed building was the birthplace of Dr. Samuel Johnson in 1709. He lived here for much of his first 27 years, long before he became the "Great Man of Letters" in London.


The ground floor where you enter wasn't just a hallway; it was his father Michael’s bookshop. Growing up surrounded by thousands of books gave Samuel an education no school could match. But life here was tough. The family was often in debt, and Samuel himself was a sickly child.

 If you look at portraits of Johnson inside, you’ll notice deep scarring on his face. As a baby, he contracted scrofula—a skin disease known as the "King’s Evil." Back then, people believed only the touch of a monarch could cure it. At just two years old, Samuel was taken all the way to London to be "touched" by Queen Anne. It didn’t work, but he kept the silver "touch piece" she gave him for the rest of his life.

Johnson is world-famous for his Dictionary of the English Language. Now, a common myth is that it was the first English dictionary—it wasn't! But it was the first to use over 114,000 literary quotes to show how words were actually used. He wrote almost the entire thing himself in just nine years—a task that took forty French scholars nearly a century to complete for their own language.

Step inside to see his tea set, his massive armchair, and the very room where he was born. As you explore the five floors, remember his own words: "Curiosity is, in great and generous minds, the first passion and the last."
